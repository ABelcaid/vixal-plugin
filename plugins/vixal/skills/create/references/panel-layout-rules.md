# Panel Layout Rules

Use this reference when planning a comic page, a panel breakdown, a multi-panel generation prompt, or chapter pages. Order of decisions: story beat, then pacing, then panel hierarchy, then layout, then framing. Never pick a grid first and fill it.

## Page Composition

- Give every page a visual hierarchy. Decide the most important beat and give it the largest or most prominent panel. Do not default to equal-sized panels.
- Panel size reflects importance, duration, scale, or emotion. Small panels for quick actions, reactions, and details; large panels for reveals, establishing shots, major actions, location changes, emotional peaks, and climaxes.
- Every panel has a storytelling purpose. Cut panels that repeat the same information.
- A page usually moves through an entry panel, development, a focal beat, and an exit (reveal, transition, or hook). When the story continues, the last panel pulls the reader to the next page.
- Panel count follows pacing. These are guides, not formulas:
  - quiet emotional page: 2 to 5 panels
  - standard narrative page: about 4 to 7
  - fast sequence: several smaller panels
  - major reveal: 1 to 3 large panels

## Shots And Continuity

- Vary framing by story need: wide or establishing for place and geography, medium for interaction, close-up for emotion, extreme close-up for a key detail, over-the-shoulder for conversation or confrontation, POV when subjectivity matters. Use low or high angles only when they serve the beat, not to look dynamic.
- Entering a new location: consider an establishing panel before close dialogue or action, unless withholding the place is the point.
- Keep screen direction, eyelines, and the positions of doors, furniture, weapons, props, and landmarks consistent. Characters do not swap sides between consecutive panels without a shown reason.
- In action, clarity beats spectacle. Establish where everyone is first, show key actions clearly, and use action, then consequence, then reaction. One strong silhouette beats clutter; do not fill every panel with speed lines, debris, or explosions.
- In emotional scenes, prioritize faces, body language, and reaction shots; keep backgrounds quiet.
- Give major reveals space: panel size, page position, framing, and negative space. Never hide a big surprise in a small panel among equals.

## Dialogue Space And Rhythm

- Leave negative space for balloons where dialogue exists, placed along the reading order. Keep faces, hands, key objects, and actions clear of where balloons will go.
- Do not overload one panel with dialogue; split a long exchange across panels.
- Several small panels speed time up; a large panel slows it; white space creates pause, isolation, or transition. Equal panels are fine for a deliberate calm rhythm, not as the default.

## By Tradition

Manga:
- Right-to-left. Roughly 3 to 8 panels, decided by pacing.
- Asymmetrical and dynamic but readable. Large impact or emotional panels used selectively; narrow panels for timing beats and details.
- Overlaps and angled borders only when they add energy or meaning. Not every page should be chaotic.

Western comics:
- Left-to-right, top-to-bottom. Roughly 3 to 7 panels, decided by pacing.
- Start from a readable grid; break it for a narrative reason. Clear gutters. Splash panels selectively.
- Avoid making every page a plain 2x3 or 3x2 grid.

Manhwa (vertical scroll):
- Not a printed page cut into boxes. Vertical spacing controls timing; bigger gaps for pauses, suspense, time jumps, or reveals.
- Wide or large panels for establishing and dramatic beats, alternating with smaller conversation and reaction panels.

Webtoon:
- Built for phone reading: usually one major visual beat at a time, with readable faces and text at mobile size.
- No tiny multi-panel print grids. Long vertical panels only for movement, height, falling, travel, scale, or suspense.
